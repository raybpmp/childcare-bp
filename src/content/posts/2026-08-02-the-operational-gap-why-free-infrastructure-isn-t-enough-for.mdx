The recent struggle of the Oklahoma Office of Management and Enterprise Services (OMES) to find a private operator for a state-funded childcare center is a masterclass in the disconnect between capital investment and operational reality. The state has invested over $45 million into renovating the Kelley Building and Annex to create a five-story facility capable of serving 250 children. On paper, this looks like a massive win for state employees and the local community. In practice, it has resulted in two failed requests for proposals (RFPs) and a total lack of interested bidders.

The core of the problem lies in a fundamental misunderstanding of operational expenditure (OpEx) versus capital expenditure (CapEx). While the state provided the "shell"—the building and the renovations—it expected the private operator to shoulder 100% of the day-to-day operational costs. In the current economic climate of 2026, this model is not just risky; it is mathematically impossible for most providers.

## The CapEx vs. OpEx Fallacy

In many government projects, there is a tendency to believe that removing the barrier of real estate (CapEx) solves the problem of service delivery. By providing a renovated, high-end facility, the state believed it was offering an irresistible incentive. However, in the childcare industry, the building is often the smallest part of the long-term financial burden.

The operational costs of running a center for 250 children are staggering. When a state agency asks a provider to cover staffing, furnishing, play equipment, and liability insurance without providing an operational subsidy, they are essentially asking a business to gamble on a high-overhead model with a capped revenue stream.

For a facility of this scale, the operational budget is dominated by labor. In 2026, the childcare industry is facing a severe labor crisis. Wages have risen to keep pace with inflation, but tuition rates cannot rise at the same speed because parents simply cannot afford it. This creates a "margin squeeze" where the cost of providing quality care exceeds the market rate that parents are willing or able to pay.

## Breaking Down the Operational Costs of a 250-Child Center

To understand why providers are ignoring the Oklahoma RFP, we have to look at the actual numbers involved in operating a large-scale center.

### 1. Staffing Ratios and Payroll
Childcare is not a scalable business in the way software is; it is a human-centric service with strict legal ratios. Depending on the age of the children, these ratios are non-negotiable:
*   **Infants (0-12 months):** Typically 1:4. For a center serving a significant number of infants, you need a massive staff.
*   **Toddlers (1-2 years):** Typically 1:6 or 1:8.
*   **Preschoolers (3-5 years):** Typically 1:10 or 1:12.

To serve 250 children across these age groups, a provider would need approximately 30 to 45 full-time equivalent (FTE) staff members, including lead teachers, assistants, and administrative support. At a competitive 2026 wage—necessary to attract talent away from retail or fast food—payroll easily becomes the largest line item, often consuming 70% to 80% of total revenue.

### 2. Liability and Insurance
Operating a five-story facility increases the complexity of risk management. Liability insurance for childcare is among the most expensive in the service sector. When you add the scale of 250 children, the premiums for general liability, professional liability, and workers' compensation become a significant monthly drain on cash flow.

### 3. Equipment and Consumables
The state provided the building, but the provider must provide the "guts." This includes:
*   **Age-appropriate furniture:** Cribs, high chairs, and tables.
*   **Educational materials:** Toys, books, and art supplies.
*   **Safety equipment:** Specialized flooring, gated areas, and monitoring systems.
*   **Consumables:** Diapers, wipes, cleaning supplies, and food for 250 children daily.

For a new center, the initial "fit-out" cost for equipment can run into the hundreds of thousands of dollars, even if the walls and floors are already there.

## The Revenue Paradox: The Affordability Gap

The most critical failure in the Oklahoma model is the assumption that revenue will cover these costs. In the childcare industry, there is a known "affordability gap."

The cost to provide high-quality care (paying teachers a living wage, maintaining safety standards, providing nutritious meals) is significantly higher than what the average family can afford to pay. If a provider raises tuition to cover their operational costs, they lose their customers. If they keep tuition low to attract families, they operate at a loss.

Rep. Suzanne Schreiber correctly pointed out that families cannot pay what it takes to build out and staff a new center. When the state removes the building cost but leaves the operational cost, they are still leaving the provider in a position where the business plan is unsustainable.

## Why Banks Won't Finance the Gap

The news report mentions that providers would struggle to obtain bank loans to pay for operations. This is a crucial point for anyone studying business operations. 

Banks lend based on the ability to service debt through consistent cash flow. A business plan for a childcare center that relies solely on tuition—without government operational subsidies—often shows a razor-thin margin or a net loss. When a bank sees a business model where the primary expense (labor) is rising faster than the primary revenue (tuition), they view the venture as high-risk.

Furthermore, the provider does not own the building. Because the state owns the Kelley Building, the provider has no collateral. They cannot leverage the real estate to secure a loan. They are essentially tenants in a high-cost operation with no equity to fall back on.

## Lessons for Future Public-Private Partnerships (PPP)

The Oklahoma situation serves as a warning for other municipalities and states attempting to solve the childcare crisis. To make these facilities viable, the operational model must change.

### 1. Shift from CapEx to OpEx Support
Investing in buildings is the "easy" part of government spending because it is a one-time appropriation. However, childcare is a service, not a product. Future projects should include "operational bridge funding"—subsidies that cover a percentage of payroll for the first 3-5 years until the center reaches full capacity and stabilizes its revenue.

### 2. Direct Staffing Subsidies
Instead of asking the provider to manage the entire payroll, the state could offer direct subsidies for childcare worker wages. By treating childcare workers as essential infrastructure employees (similar to how some states treat preschool teachers), the government can lower the operational burden on the private provider.

### 3. Integrated Funding Streams
A successful model would integrate state-funded vouchers, employer-sponsored childcare credits, and private tuition. If the state guarantees a certain level of reimbursement per child, the provider has a guaranteed revenue stream that they can take to a bank to secure financing.

## Childcare as Essential Infrastructure

The central argument made by Rep. Schreiber is that childcare should be viewed as a necessity, much like healthcare or oil and gas infrastructure. From an operational standpoint, this is the only way forward.

When we build a road, the government pays for the construction (CapEx) and the maintenance (OpEx). When we fund a public health clinic, we don't just provide the building and hope a private doctor can make a profit on a population that can't afford the bills. We provide operational funding because the *existence* of the service is the goal, not the *profitability* of the provider.

The Oklahoma failure proves that "free" real estate is not an incentive if the cost of staying open exceeds the potential for profit. For the third attempt at the RFP to succeed, the state may need to move beyond the role of a landlord and become a true operational partner.

## Summary of the Operational Failure

| Element | State Provided (CapEx) | Provider Required (OpEx) | Result |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Facility** | $45M Renovated Building | N/A | Success |
| **Labor** | Nothing | 30-45 FTE Staff | Failure (High Cost) |
| **Equipment** | Nothing | Full Fit-out | Failure (High Upfront) |
| **Insurance** | Nothing | High-risk Liability | Failure (Recurring Cost) |
| **Revenue** | Nothing | Market-rate Tuition | Failure (Insufficient) |

Ultimately, the Oklahoma case study highlights a critical lesson in business operations: the cost of entry is not the same as the cost of existence. A beautiful building is useless if the math of the daily operation doesn't add up. For the childcare industry to survive and expand, the focus must shift from the bricks and mortar to the people and the payroll.
